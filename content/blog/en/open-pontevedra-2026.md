## The Setting: Pontevedra International Open 2026

From **July 25 to 30, 2026**, the city of **Pontevedra** (Galicia, Spain) hosted an international chess Open with over 100 players from across Europe and Latin America. FIDE classical time control, 9 rounds, a strong field.

My goals: enjoy playing, stop wrecking equal positions, and be solid.

## Round 1 — The English, the Bluff, and the Spectator Bishop

**Date:** Friday, July 25, 2026

The first round of a tournament is always a bit special. No matter how many events you've played, there's always that slight tension when you sit down at the board. The reflexes come back quickly... but never on the very first move.

Across from me, a young Portuguese player rated around 1800 Elo. I'm expecting an energetic player, ready to calculate the slightest complication. Which is exactly why I have zero interest in turning this first game into a tactics contest.

So I decide to play the **English**. A first for me in classical chess. The idea is simple: get a strategic position, keep control, and impose a battle of plans rather than a battle of variations.

My opponent, however, doesn't seem familiar with the concept of thinking. He plays almost every move instantly. After a few minutes, I start looking at his clock more often than the board. Either he knows everything by heart... or he's one hell of a bluffer.

I think I've got a slight strategic edge. I've just played **b5**, expecting to reach a beautiful position. I love what I see: White seems to control the key squares, the pieces are harmonious, and I'm quietly getting ready to roll out my plan.

<div class="diagram-container" style="text-align:center;margin:2rem 0;">
  <img src="/images/blog/pontevedra-2026/ronde1-diagramme1.svg" alt="Round 1 — position after b5, White is confident" style="max-width:480px;width:100%;border-radius:0.75rem;box-shadow:0 2px 12px rgba(0,0,0,0.08);" />
  <p style="font-size:0.9rem;color:#6b7280;margin-top:0.5rem;font-style:italic;">Position after 11.b5 — White is satisfied... a bit too soon.</p>
</div>

And then comes the first bucket of cold water. Without a second's hesitation, he plays:

> **...Nd4!**

The move is strong. Very strong. And above all, I hadn't seriously considered it — because it gives up the exchange. I probably should have played **Nd2**, which should have kept a small edge, but in the moment, the capture was impossible to resist. The problem is that this sacrifice completely changes the dynamics of the position.

A few moves later, he finds the logical follow-up:

> **...d5!!**

<div class="diagram-container" style="text-align:center;margin:2rem 0;">
  <img src="/images/blog/pontevedra-2026/ronde1-diagramme2.svg" alt="Round 1 — after ...d5, Black seizes the initiative" style="max-width:480px;width:100%;border-radius:0.75rem;box-shadow:0 2px 12px rgba(0,0,0,0.08);" />
  <p style="font-size:0.9rem;color:#6b7280;margin-top:0.5rem;font-style:italic;">There's only one move for Black — and he found it.</p>
</div>

And at that point, there's no sugarcoating it. The black knight is parked on d5 like it owns the place, the bishop pair springs to life in one go, while my poor bishop on c1 discovers an unexpected calling: watching the game up close without ever participating.

Within minutes, I go from "satisfied player" to "proud owner of the worst bishop on the board."

The decision that follows is somewhat counterintuitive: **give back the exchange** in order to win the c3 pawn under favorable conditions. This was clearly not the script I had imagined ten moves earlier, but strategically, it's the price to pay for a playable position.

My opponent chooses to enter an **opposite-colored bishop endgame** a pawn down, thinking he can hold easily. But that's underestimating White's center — and especially the **g4** breakthrough.

<div class="diagram-container" style="text-align:center;margin:2rem 0;">
  <img src="/images/blog/pontevedra-2026/ronde1-diagramme3.svg" alt="Round 1 — opposite-colored bishop endgame" style="max-width:480px;width:100%;border-radius:0.75rem;box-shadow:0 2px 12px rgba(0,0,0,0.08);" />
  <p style="font-size:0.9rem;color:#6b7280;margin-top:0.5rem;font-style:italic;">Opposite-colored bishops — but White's center will make the difference.</p>
</div>

The plan has been clear for several moves: install a pawn on d5, gradually improve the pieces, then prepare the **g4** push.

<div class="diagram-container" style="text-align:center;margin:2rem 0;">
  <img src="/images/blog/pontevedra-2026/ronde1-diagramme4.svg" alt="Round 1 — the g4 breakthrough decides the endgame" style="max-width:480px;width:100%;border-radius:0.75rem;box-shadow:0 2px 12px rgba(0,0,0,0.08);" />
  <p style="font-size:0.9rem;color:#6b7280;margin-top:0.5rem;font-style:italic;">The g4 breakthrough — from here, Black's defense crumbles.</p>
</div>

The g4 breakthrough practically decides the endgame. If he takes on g4, I create a passed pawn with h5 that becomes extremely hard to stop. If he declines the capture, I can calmly prepare the e6 break, which completely wrecks his structure.

**Takeaway:** this first round is a pretty good summary of what a tournament is like — for a few moves, I thought I was way cleverer than I actually was. Fortunately, endgames sometimes forgive the opening's excess optimism. And in opposite-colored bishop endings, remember this: if you get two connected passed pawns and there are still a few pawns on the board, that's often enough to make the difference.

## Round 2 — When a 17-Year-Old Makes You Want to Take a Nap

**Date:** Saturday, July 26, 2026

First double round of the tournament. Double rounds aren't my favorite — they're exhausting, physically and mentally. Two classical games in one day means managing your energy as much as your moves.

Morning round. I'm a bit groggy, but ready to fight.

Across from me, a young Spanish prodigy, 17 years old. Peak rating around 2420, currently listed at 2365. The kind of profile that only goes up and gently reminds you that at 17, you were mostly busy missing three-move forks.

Looking at the database, I expect 1.e4 and a tense Sicilian — the natural playground of an ambitious young player looking for a fight.

Surprise: he goes for **1.b3**. Not at all what I'd prepared for. Fortunately, I know the theory in these structures fairly well and I reel off my lines for a good while. We reach a relatively equal, simplified, clean position.

<div class="diagram-container" style="text-align:center;margin:2rem 0;">
  <img src="/images/blog/pontevedra-2026/ronde2-diagramme1.svg" alt="Round 2 — equal position but slightly uncomfortable with knight vs bishop" style="max-width:480px;width:100%;border-radius:0.75rem;box-shadow:0 2px 12px rgba(0,0,0,0.08);" />
  <p style="font-size:0.9rem;color:#6b7280;margin-top:0.5rem;font-style:italic;">Position after 22 moves — equal on paper, but the bishop is taunting the knight.</p>
</div>

In practice, I feel slightly worse: he has the bishop against my knight, and in this kind of open endgame, the bishop tends to stroll around like a tourist on vacation while the knight desperately looks for a stable square. But the position is very holdable, and I'm happy to have equalized against a 2365.

The problem is that I know exactly what's coming. This kid isn't going to rush. On the contrary — he'll play very slowly, avoid pawn pushes, set subtle traps, and pounce on the slightest opportunity. The kind of silent, almost polite grind where every move looks harmless but the pressure never lets up.

<div class="diagram-container" style="text-align:center;margin:2rem 0;">
  <img src="/images/blog/pontevedra-2026/ronde2-diagramme2.svg" alt="Round 2 — the white bishop has settled on c4, Black hasn't moved" style="max-width:480px;width:100%;border-radius:0.75rem;box-shadow:0 2px 12px rgba(0,0,0,0.08);" />
  <p style="font-size:0.9rem;color:#6b7280;margin-top:0.5rem;font-style:italic;">29 moves later — he has a plan, I have... a position.</p>
</div>

Seven moves later, the picture is crystal clear. His bishop has settled on c4 like it booked a five-star Airbnb, and he's about to launch kingside pawn pushes to create weaknesses. On my side, I haven't budged an inch. My position holds, but it holds like a student holds a presentation he didn't prepare — technically standing, but without much conviction.

And then I find **...g5** — an important defensive move that finally gives my knight some life. Suddenly, it has prospects, squares, almost a reason to exist.

<div class="diagram-container" style="text-align:center;margin:2rem 0;">
  <img src="/images/blog/pontevedra-2026/ronde2-diagramme3.svg" alt="Round 2 — g5, the knight comes back to life" style="max-width:480px;width:100%;border-radius:0.75rem;box-shadow:0 2px 12px rgba(0,0,0,0.08);" />
  <p style="font-size:0.9rem;color:#6b7280;margin-top:0.5rem;font-style:italic;">...g5 — the knight finally finds a reason to exist.</p>
</div>

Then follows **Re4 Rd4** — and he surprises me: he sacrifices the exchange. A move I clearly underestimated. The kind of decision that reminds you that 2400-level juniors aren't there just to make up numbers.

<div class="diagram-container" style="text-align:center;margin:2rem 0;">
  <img src="/images/blog/pontevedra-2026/ronde2-diagramme4.svg" alt="Round 2 — unexpected exchange sacrifice" style="max-width:480px;width:100%;border-radius:0.75rem;box-shadow:0 2px 12px rgba(0,0,0,0.08);" />
  <p style="font-size:0.9rem;color:#6b7280;margin-top:0.5rem;font-style:italic;">Rxe6! — yet another exchange sacrifice. A recurring theme, apparently.</p>
</div>

> 33.Rxe6+ fxe6 34.Rxe6+ Kg7 35.h5 Rdxc4 36.bxc4 Rxc4 37.Rg6+ Kh7 38.Rxb6 Rxa4

Fortunately, after all the captures, I can simply give back the exchange on c4 and reach an easily drawn rook endgame. "Easily drawn" — except you still have to hold it for 30 moves against a kid who refuses to sign the draw sheet the way a teenager refuses to clean their room. But the position holds, and the draw comes through.

My opponent was serious, focused, and played exactly the way an ambitious young player should play against a tired old FM: patiently, methodically, no gifts. Nothing spectacular, but solid. The kind of game you don't lose, and where you still think "thank God I know my endgames."

**Takeaway:** against a young talent who grinds, don't try to shine — surviving is already a result. And always keep an eye on exchange sacrifices: this tournament has apparently decided I'll face one per game.

## Round 3 — The Zombie Draw

**Date:** Saturday, July 26, 2026 (afternoon)

Second game of the double round. Across from me, an **experienced International Master** from Uruguay, rated 2310. Two tired players, one board, and a shared desire to above all not do anything stupid.

I get the theory wrong fairly early — the kind of slip you'd never make fresh and rested, but that happens naturally when your brain is running in power-saving mode. He equalizes without breaking a sweat. On my side, I flatten the position as much as possible: if I can't win, at least I can make sure I don't lose anything.

He offers a draw pretty quickly. I don't need long to think about it.

**Takeaway:** sometimes, the best game is the one you don't really play. A clean half-point against an IM at the end of a double round is a result you take without asking questions — and forget immediately so you can go to sleep.

## Round 4 — When You Think You're Tal

**Date:** Sunday, July 27, 2026

I arrive motivated. The tournament is going well — 2/3 with a draw against an IM, now's the time to push for the full point.

A **Najdorf** — a first for me in classical chess. I target him on a line with g3 and get a good position.

<div class="diagram-container" style="text-align:center;margin:2rem 0;">

  <p style="font-size:0.9rem;color:#6b7280;margin-top:0.5rem;font-style:italic;">Position after 15 moves — White is well set up in the Najdorf.</p>
</div>

I find the creative (for me) **Bd8!** with the idea of activating it via b6. One of those moves that makes you feel like a genius for about five minutes. He goes for the most aggressive line — and I counter with the sequence **d5! exd5 e4!** which blows the position wide open in my favor.

<div class="diagram-container" style="text-align:center;margin:2rem 0;">
  <img src="/images/blog/pontevedra-2026/ronde4-diagramme2.svg" alt="Round 4 — position before d5! exd5 e4!" style="max-width:480px;width:100%;border-radius:0.75rem;box-shadow:0 2px 12px rgba(0,0,0,0.08);" />

After the tactical flurry, I reach a promising position. And that's where things go off the rails: with 10 minutes on my clock versus 15 on his, I almost instantly go for the wildly ambitious **...fxh3**, channeling my inner Tal. The problem is that I completely overlooked the simple sequence **...Qc7, Rd1, Bg4!!** which wins by force — cleanly, no drama, no dubious sacrifice.

</div>

We reach a position where he's almost completely paralyzed. The kind of diagram you proudly show your friends saying "look how winning this was."

<div class="diagram-container" style="text-align:center;margin:2rem 0;">
  <img src="/images/blog/pontevedra-2026/ronde4-diagramme4.svg" alt="Round 4 — near-winning position, opponent paralyzed" style="max-width:480px;width:100%;border-radius:0.75rem;box-shadow:0 2px 12px rgba(0,0,0,0.08);" />
  <p style="font-size:0.9rem;color:#6b7280;margin-top:0.5rem;font-style:italic;">Almost paralyzed — but "almost" doesn't count in chess.</p>
</div>

But the Colombian IM finds all the right defensive moves — coolly, methodically, without panicking. And on my side, the moves become extremely hard to find. With 10 minutes on the clock, I can no longer calculate the continuations properly. Lack of clarity, and he ends up winning.

**Takeaway:** when you're dominating, stabilize and convert — don't improvise Tal-like sacrifices. Especially when you're not Tal.

## Round 5 — The Old Fox's Trap

**Date:** Monday, July 28, 2026

Another double round. After yesterday's loss, I need to bounce back — but without wasting energy. Against a young Spaniard rated 2076, I pull out **b3 Bb2** to dodge any preparation. And I blunder a pawn. That's what happens when you play the opening on autopilot.

Except the position is actually less dire than I thought during the game.

He goes for a very direct plan, and I set a little trap. Here, he plays **Re8** with tempo — and I don't hesitate.

> **Rxa5!**

A move that at least rattles him psychologically, even if objectively the position is roughly equal.

He doesn't find the best defense, and we reach a position where I spend **35 minutes** trying to find a win. After **Rh8, Kg6!!** holds for Black and I can't find anything clear. I end up going for **Re6** and keep pressing.

## Round 6 — Ground Down by a 13-Year-Old

**Date:** Monday, July 28, 2026 (afternoon)

Second game of the double round. Across from me, a 13-year-old Brazilian rated 2350. Thirteen. The age when some kids discover chess; this one is already crushing FMs in international tournaments.

Very little time to prepare between the two rounds. I go for an **Accelerated Dragon** that morphs into an Alapin. I've just played **d4** and he chooses the very solid **Ne2**.

<div class="diagram-container" style="text-align:center;margin:2rem 0;">
  <img src="/images/blog/pontevedra-2026/ronde6-diagramme1.svg" alt="Round 6 — Accelerated Dragon turned Alapin" style="max-width:480px;width:100%;border-radius:0.75rem;box-shadow:0 2px 12px rgba(0,0,0,0.08);" />
  <p style="font-size:0.9rem;color:#6b7280;margin-top:0.5rem;font-style:italic;">After Ne2 — solid, not spectacular, but effective.</p>
</div>

I don't miss my chance to simplify and go for the sequence **Nxe5 Nxe5 Bxe5 Bxe5 Qa5 Qd2 Qxe5 Qxd4 Qxd4 Nxd4**. We reach a stripped-down position.

<div class="diagram-container" style="text-align:center;margin:2rem 0;">
  <img src="/images/blog/pontevedra-2026/ronde6-diagramme2.svg" alt="Round 6 — simplified position after the exchanges" style="max-width:480px;width:100%;border-radius:0.75rem;box-shadow:0 2px 12px rgba(0,0,0,0.08);" />

The engine suggests **Nf6 e5 b5** to nearly equalize, but I find that line too tense. I go for **Nh6 Bc4 Rf8!** with the idea of activating the rook on the f-file. I don't have the nerve to play a5 and choose the very solid **Be6**, because I correctly calculate that **Bxb7** lets me trade everything off. He chooses to exchange bishops.

After a few trades, we reach this position that he fully intends to grind.

It's hot in the playing hall. I look tired. He has the energy of a kid who just discovered an all-you-can-eat candy buffet. He's a bit imprecise, and I reach a position that starts looking threatening for Black.

But I'm exhausted and starting to lose clarity. Here, I see **e5** — but I don't trust it. The kind of moment where you need to trust your instinct, and fatigue won't let you.

<div class="diagram-container" style="text-align:center;margin:2rem 0;">

Hats off to him. At 13, grinding down a tired FM in a sweltering hall is exactly what you need to do to improve. As for me, I mostly should have drunk more water.

That said, with a name like that — **Casalaspro** — I probably should have taken the hint straight off the pairing sheet. I'd likely have been more than fine against *Casals-amateur*, or even *Casals-semi-pro*… but *Casals-**pro***, well, that's immediately far too serious for me.

**Takeaway:** physical fitness isn't a bonus in tournament chess — it's a prerequisite. When the body quits, clarity follows, and the moves you see, you no longer dare to play.

## Round 7 — The Win That Won't Come

**Date:** Tuesday, July 29, 2026

Clear objective: 3/3 over the last three rounds to scrape some Elo points. Across from me, a 14-year-old Spaniard. I target his French Defense — he plays a very particular setup against the King's Indian Attack, and I find a little idea to force him into a new scheme. I'm counting on his lack of experience to outplay him.

<div class="diagram-container" style="text-align:center;margin:2rem 0;">

The preparation pays off: I have **one hour** against **20 minutes** on his clock. And that's when I sink 40 minutes trying to make **Nxh6** work — which doesn't work. The kind of moment where you think "I've got time, might as well look for the killer blow." Except the killer blow doesn't exist.

## Lessons Learned

**4.5/9.** Let's be honest: I'm disappointed. Not by the level of the opposition — the field was strong and I held my own against IMs and FMs — but by the points left on the table. A Round 4 wrecked by an excess of creativity, a Round 7 where the win slipped through my fingers, and two losses (R6, R9) where fatigue spoke louder than the board. With a bit more clarity in the key moments, this tournament could have been a 6/9. But "could have been" doesn't earn Elo points.

Here's what I'm taking home:

1. **Stamina** — this is THE project. The double rounds ate me alive. From Round 6 onward, my move quality drops with every game. I need to work on my physical fitness as much as my openings. Running, sleeping, eating properly — the kind of advice you give to others without ever following it yourself.

2. **Endgames, endgames, endgames** — Round 7 is a textbook case. Three missed wins in a single game. The positions were there, the technique wasn't. Program: one hour of endgames per week, minimum. Rook and pawns, bishop and pawns, the classics you think you know and rediscover at every tournament.

3. **Fighting spirit vs. clarity** — Rounds 4 and 8 tell two opposite stories. In R4, I think I'm Tal and wreck a winning position. In R8, I stay patient and convert cleanly. The lesson is clear: ambition, yes, but channeled. When the positional move wins, don't go looking for the sacrifice.

4. **Trust your instinct** — in R6, I see e5 and don't play it. In R7, I sense the win and hesitate. When intuition speaks, you have to listen — especially when the clock no longer leaves time to calculate.

5. **Preparation pays off** — Rounds 2 and 7 prove it. When I arrive with a 30-minute clock advantage thanks to prep, I'm a different player. That needs to become systematic.

6. **Sleep** — I'm only half joking. Round 9 is the direct consequence of a bad night. No sleep, no clarity, no result. It's as simple as that.

**Final score: 4.5/9** — disappointing, but instructive. No time to dwell on it: I'm heading straight to **Badalona**. Rest will have to wait.
